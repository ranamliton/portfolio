Rooftop and Surface Garden Soils in Bangladesh Harbor Diverse Resistome Profiles

Home / Publications / Publication Details

Overview

This study employed shotgun whole-metagenome sequencing (WMS) to characterize the soil resistome from 27 soil samples representing rooftop and surface gardens in Dhaka and Gazipur districts, Bangladesh. The research reveals diverse antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiles and highlights the critical role of urban garden soils as reservoirs of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), with significant implications for public health and environmental safety.

Introduction

Urban agriculture is expanding rapidly, particularly in densely populated cities like Dhaka, Bangladesh. Rooftop and surface gardens have become essential components of urban food production systems. However, the diversity, composition, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiles (i.e., resistome) of microbial communities in these urban garden soils remain poorly understood. This study addresses this knowledge gap by providing the first comprehensive characterization of the resistome in Bangladeshi urban garden soils using advanced metagenomic approaches.

Soil microorganisms are essential for nutrient cycling and plant health but are increasingly threatened by urban pollution, waste, and farming practices, which may also contribute to the spread of antibiotic resistance genes. Understanding the resistome profiles of urban garden soils is crucial for assessing potential public health risks associated with urban agriculture.

Study Design and Methodology

The study analyzed 27 soil samples collected from rooftop and surface gardens in Dhaka and Gazipur districts, Bangladesh, comprising:

  • Dhaka Rooftop Gardens (DRG): 7 samples
  • Dhaka Surface Gardens (DSG): 6 samples
  • Gazipur Rooftop Gardens (GRG): 8 samples
  • Gazipur Surface Gardens (GSG): 6 samples

These gardens, located 15–20 km apart (23.72–23.99°N, 90.40-90.42°E), experience a tropical monsoon climate with 1,875 mm average rainfall, 26.1°C mean annual temperature, and 65.8% humidity. Composite soil samples (0–5 cm depth) were aseptically collected during April–July 2024.

Shotgun whole-metagenome sequencing (WMS) was performed using Illumina NovaSeq 6000, generating 1.34 billion reads (avg. 49.8 M/sample). The CZ ID pipeline was used for taxonomic and functional analysis, and the MEGARes database was employed for resistome profiling.

Key Findings

Bacterial Community Composition

WMS revealed distinct microbiome profiles across garden types:

  • Firmicutes dominated (65.3%–83.2% relative abundance), followed by Proteobacteria (3.4%–24.9%)
  • Bacillus species demonstrated ecological dominance (>53% relative abundance) across all metagenomes
  • DRG showed high Bacillus paralicheniformis (28.3%) and Bacillus licheniformis (25.2%)
  • DSG exhibited greater diversity, with dominant species including Brevibacillus agri (12.1%) and Flavobacterium thermophilum (11.4%)
  • GSG uniquely hosted Rhodanobacter lindaniclasticus and Acidovorax kalamii

Resistome Diversity and Composition

The study identified a diverse array of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) across all sample types:

  • Multi-drug resistance genes were the most abundant class of ARGs detected
  • Bacitracin, macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS), and sulfonamide resistance genes were prevalent across all garden types
  • Tetracycline and beta-lactam resistance genes showed significant variation between rooftop and surface gardens
  • Co-occurrence patterns revealed potential co-selection of antibiotic, metal, and biocide resistance

Location-Specific Patterns

Significant differences were observed between Dhaka and Gazipur samples:

  • Dhaka rooftop gardens harbored distinct resistome profiles compared to surface gardens
  • Gazipur samples showed unique ARG signatures, potentially influenced by local agricultural practices
  • Urbanization gradients correlated with resistome diversity and ARG abundance

Mobile Genetic Elements

The study detected various mobile genetic elements (MGEs) associated with ARG dissemination:

  • Integrons and transposases were identified in multiple samples
  • Plasmid-mediated resistance markers suggested potential horizontal gene transfer
  • Class 1 integron-integrase genes indicated anthropogenic pollution influence

Implications and Significance

This research has critical implications for multiple stakeholders:

  • Public Health: Urban garden soils may serve as reservoirs for ARGs, potentially contributing to the spread of antimicrobial resistance through food chain contamination and direct human contact
  • Urban Agriculture Policy: Findings support the need for soil quality monitoring and safe gardening practices in urban settings
  • Environmental Management: The study highlights the importance of managing urban soil microbiomes to prevent ARG dissemination
  • One Health Approach: Results underscore the interconnectedness of environmental, animal, and human health in the context of antimicrobial resistance

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of the resistome in Bangladeshi urban garden soils, revealing diverse and abundant antimicrobial resistance gene profiles. The dominance of Bacillus species and the presence of multiple ARG classes highlight the need for continued surveillance and risk assessment in urban agricultural systems. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence on the environmental dimensions of antimicrobial resistance and support the development of evidence-based policies for safe urban agriculture practices.

The research emphasizes the critical need for integrated approaches combining soil microbiome monitoring, sustainable urban farming practices, and public health surveillance to mitigate the risks associated with environmental antimicrobial resistance.
