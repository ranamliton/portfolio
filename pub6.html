Molecular Detection and Genomic Characterization of Foodborne Viruses in Environmental and Food Samples from Bangladesh

Home / Publications / Project Details

Project Overview

This study investigated the prevalence, molecular characteristics, and genomic diversity of foodborne viruses in environmental and food samples from Bangladesh. Using molecular detection methods and genomic sequencing, the research identified key viral pathogens including norovirus, rotavirus, hepatitis A virus, and hepatitis E virus in various food matrices and environmental sources. The findings provide critical insights into viral contamination patterns in the food chain and highlight the need for improved food safety surveillance in Bangladesh.

Introduction

Foodborne viruses are a significant cause of gastroenteritis worldwide, with norovirus, rotavirus, hepatitis A virus (HAV), and hepatitis E virus (HEV) being the most common culprits. These viruses are primarily transmitted through the fecal-oral route, often via contaminated food and water. In developing countries like Bangladesh, where sanitation infrastructure may be inadequate and food handling practices sometimes fall short of international standards, the risk of foodborne viral transmission is particularly high.

Despite the significant public health burden, data on foodborne viruses in Bangladesh remains limited. Most surveillance efforts have focused on bacterial pathogens, while viral agents are often underdiagnosed and underreported. This gap in knowledge hampers the development of effective prevention and control strategies. Understanding the molecular epidemiology and genomic diversity of these viruses is essential for tracking transmission routes, identifying outbreak sources, and implementing targeted interventions.

This study aimed to detect and characterize foodborne viruses in various environmental and food samples from Bangladesh, providing the first comprehensive molecular survey of viral contamination in the local food chain. The research employed sensitive molecular detection methods combined with genomic sequencing to identify circulating genotypes and assess genetic diversity.

Study Design and Methodology

The study collected and analyzed samples from multiple sources across Bangladesh:

  • Food Samples: Raw vegetables, fruits, shellfish, and street food items
  • Environmental Samples: Surface water, irrigation water, and sewage samples
  • Clinical Correlates: Stool samples from gastroenteritis patients for comparison

Samples were processed for viral nucleic acid extraction using established protocols. Molecular detection was performed using:

  • RT-PCR: Reverse transcription PCR for RNA virus detection
  • qPCR: Real-time quantitative PCR for viral load estimation
  • Genomic Sequencing: Next-generation sequencing for full-length or partial genome characterization
  • Phylogenetic Analysis: Sequence alignment and tree construction for genotype determination

Key Findings

Virus Detection and Prevalence

Molecular screening revealed significant viral contamination:

  • Norovirus: Detected in a significant proportion of food and environmental samples, with GII genogroup predominating
  • Rotavirus: Identified across multiple sample types, predominantly G1P[8] and G2P[4] genotypes
  • Hepatitis A Virus: Found in shellfish and water samples, indicating fecal contamination of aquatic environments
  • Hepatitis E Virus: Detected in environmental samples, with genotype 1 predominating consistent with endemic transmission
  • Co-infections: Multiple viruses detected in several positive samples, indicating complex contamination patterns

Genomic Characterization

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis revealed:

  • Norovirus GII.4 variants: Dominant strains with evidence of recent evolution and recombination
  • Rotavirus VP7/VP4 genes: Circulating genotypes matched global patterns with local variations unique to South Asia
  • HAV subgenotype IA: Most common, with close similarity to South Asian strains from India and Nepal
  • HEV genotype 1: Predominant, consistent with endemic transmission in the region via waterborne routes
  • Genetic diversity indicated multiple transmission sources and ongoing viral evolution in the Bangladesh environment

Source Attribution and Risk Factors

Risk factor analysis identified key contamination sources:

  • Irrigation Water: Major source of pre-harvest contamination for fresh produce, particularly leafy vegetables
  • Sewage Contamination: Significant contribution to shellfish and surface water contamination in coastal areas
  • Street Food: High-risk category due to poor hygiene practices and inadequate cooking temperatures
  • Seasonal Patterns: Higher detection rates during monsoon season due to flooding and cross-contamination
  • Geographic Variation: Urban areas showed higher viral diversity than rural settings, reflecting population density effects

Implications and Significance

This research has important implications for public health and food safety:

  • First Comprehensive Survey: This study provides the first systematic molecular survey of foodborne viruses in Bangladesh, filling a critical knowledge gap in South Asian food safety
  • Food Safety Surveillance: Findings support the need for routine viral monitoring in the food chain, particularly for ready-to-eat foods and shellfish
  • Water Quality Management: Results highlight the importance of treating irrigation water and improving sewage disposal to prevent pre-harvest contamination
  • Public Health Interventions: Data supports targeted interventions during high-risk seasons and for high-risk food categories
  • Outbreak Investigation: Genomic data provides a baseline for future outbreak source tracking and molecular epidemiology
  • Policy Development: Evidence base for developing national guidelines for viral food safety standards in Bangladesh

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant contamination of food and environmental samples with foodborne viruses in Bangladesh. The detection of multiple viral pathogens, including norovirus, rotavirus, HAV, and HEV, across diverse sample types underscores the need for comprehensive food safety surveillance that includes viral agents. Genomic characterization revealed circulating genotypes consistent with both endemic transmission and potential introduction through food trade. The findings emphasize the importance of improving sanitation infrastructure, implementing Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) for irrigation water use, and enhancing food handler hygiene training. Future research should focus on quantitative risk assessment, development of preventive strategies, and establishment of routine viral monitoring programs to protect public health in Bangladesh and similar resource-limited settings.
