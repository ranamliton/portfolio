Antimicrobial Resistance, Biofilm Formation, and Virulence Determinants in Enterococcus faecalis Isolated from Cultured and Wild Fish

Home / Publications / Publication Details

Overview

This study marks the first investigation in Bangladesh focused on the molecular identification of biofilm-forming, multidrug-resistant Enterococcus faecalis from wild and cultivated fish. The research detected E. faecalis in koi fish (Anabas scandens) and catfish (Heteropneustes fossilis), followed by detection of virulence genes, antibiogram phenotype analysis, and biofilm formation assessment using the crystal violet microtiter plate (CVMP) method. The findings highlight the urgent need for a One Health approach to tackle antimicrobial resistance in aquaculture settings.

Introduction

Fish has always been an integral part of Bengali cuisine and economy, but fish can also be a potential reservoir of pathogens. Enterococcus faecalis is an opportunistic zoonotic pathogen capable of causing a range of infections in humans. Organisms with biofilm formation abilities usually use this capability to escape the effects of antibiotics. These biofilms are created when microbial cells cluster together and are encircled by extracellular polymeric materials. Biofilm formation is associated with enhanced antimicrobial resistance and persistence in the environment.

Several genes are important for biofilm development, aiding pathogenicity and controlling harsh stressors, including agg (aggregation substances), ace (adhesion of collagen), pil (adhesion on cell surface), gelE (gelatinase), fsrA, fsrB, fsrC, sprE, and cyl. Under control of the fsrABC two-component regulatory system, gelE controls cellular chain length by removing misfolded proteins from the bacterial cell surface.

The use of antibiotics in aquaculture has led to a significant rise in antimicrobial resistance worldwide, which may impact the management of human illnesses requiring antibiotic intervention. Aquaculture settings and fish markets are significant sources of antibiotic-resistant genes that can be passed from fish to people through food chains, handling practices, and environmental discharge.

Study Design and Methodology

The study collected 132 fish samples from different markets in the Mymensingh district of Bangladesh:

  • Koi fish (Anabas scandens): Wild and cultured samples
  • Catfish (Heteropneustes fossilis): Wild and cultured samples

Isolates were cultured and identified by conventional culture methods and confirmed by PCR targeting the ddlE. faecalis gene. Biofilm formation ability was assessed using the crystal violet microtiter plate (CVMP) method. Disk diffusion test was performed for antibiogram analysis, and PCR was used for virulence gene detection. The vanA and blaTEM resistance genes were specifically targeted.

Key Findings

Prevalence of E. faecalis

Among 132 fish samples analyzed:

  • 47 wild fish samples were confirmed positive for E. faecalis by PCR
  • 40 cultured fish samples were confirmed positive for E. faecalis by PCR
  • Isolates were identified from both koi fish and catfish collected across markets in Mymensingh district

Biofilm Formation

The CVMP method revealed distinct biofilm-forming capabilities between wild and cultured fish isolates:

  • 4% of isolates from cultured fish were strong biofilm formers
  • No strong biofilm producers were found from wild fish
  • Overall, 45% of isolates from wild and cultivated fish were positive for at least one biofilm-producing virulence gene
  • Biofilm formation promotes antimicrobial resistance and environmental persistence

Virulence Gene Detection

PCR screening of E. faecalis isolates revealed high virulence gene prevalence among biofilm-positive isolates:

  • gelE: 100% (gelatinase)
  • fsrC: 100% (quorum sensing)
  • ace: 95% (collagen adhesion)
  • pil: 93% (pili adhesion)
  • agg: 80% (aggregation substance)

E. faecalis from wild fish showed a higher frequency of virulence genes and biofilm-forming genes than those from cultivated fish.

Antibiogram and Multidrug Resistance

Antibiogram testing revealed alarming resistance patterns in E. faecalis from cultured fish:

  • Erythromycin: 100% resistance
  • Linezolid: 100% resistance (Reserve group antibiotic)
  • Penicillin: 100% resistance
  • Rifampicin: 100% resistance
  • Ampicillin: 82% resistance
  • Vancomycin: 7% resistance
  • Chloramphenicol: low resistance
  • All isolates sensitive to nitrofurantoin, norfloxacin, and fosfomycin

In wild fish isolates:

  • Rifampicin: 76.92% resistance
  • Erythromycin: 73.08% resistance
  • Penicillin: 69.23% resistance
  • Linezolid: 69.23% resistance
  • Ampicillin: 82.98% resistance
  • Vancomycin: 6% resistance
  • All isolates sensitive to nitrofurantoin, norfloxacin, and fosfomycin

Multidrug Resistance (MDR) Mapping

Genotypic and phenotypic analysis confirmed resistance mechanisms:

  • 100% of cultured fish isolates showed multidrug resistance
  • 73% of wild fish isolates showed multidrug resistance
  • MAR index ranged from 0.07 to 0.53 in wild fish and 0.30 to 0.53 in cultured fish
  • Cultured fish carried E. faecalis that was more highly multidrug resistant compared to wild fish

Implications and Significance

This research has critical implications for public health and aquaculture:

  • First Bangladesh Study: This is the first investigation in Bangladesh on biofilm-forming MDR E. faecalis from wild and cultivated koi and catfish using molecular evidence-based detection
  • Last-Resort Antibiotic Resistance: Detection of linezolid resistance poses serious public health risks as this is a last-resort medication for severe Gram-positive infections
  • One Health Approach: Findings recommend implementing a One Health approach with effective biosecurity and good aquaculture management
  • Food Chain Contamination: MDR E. faecalis detected in fish has the potential to end up in the food chain and transmit to humans and other hosts
  • Horizontal Gene Transfer: Many of these resistances could be transmitted horizontally to other bacterial species circulating in the same ecosystem
  • Policy Recommendations: The resistance pattern of these pathogens needs to be monitored regularly to enable the use of effective antibiotics in aquaculture if required, reducing the burden of AMR on the ecosystem

Conclusion

Enterococcus faecalis is an opportunistic zoonotic pathogen. This study revealed a high prevalence of biofilm-forming, multidrug-resistant E. faecalis in wild and cultured fish in Bangladesh, including resistance to last-resort antibiotics such as linezolid. The possibility of transfer to humans through the food chain and direct or indirect contact highlights the need for ongoing study and monitoring to protect public health and advance sustainable aquaculture techniques in Bangladesh.
